Six Degrees of Separation

The world feels small not because people are closer, but because networks obey thresholds. Add enough connections and distance collapses. What looks like coincidence, six degrees of separation, is simply what happens when social systems cross a critical point.

This idea shows up in studies of social media, professional networks, and cultural systems. One of the most famous examples is the Kevin Bacon Graph, which measures how many co-starring relationships separate any given actor from Kevin Bacon. The “Bacon number” is simply the shortest path through the network, a pop-culture illustration of the same structural principle governing social distance.

The paper, Graph Theory and the Six Degrees of Separation by Iris Heikkinen, written as an introduction to graph theory and random networks. The paper aims to show why large social systems become tightly connected. Rather than treating six degrees as a cultural curiosity, the paper frames it as a mathematical consequence of how networks form. By combining basic graph theory with probabilistic models of random graphs, it shows that global connectedness emerges from simple local rules. Six degrees is not an accident. It is the expected outcome of connectivity at scale.

Duncan Watts and Steven Strogatz are network scientists who, in the late 1990s, showed that the average number of steps between two people depends on only two things: how many people are in the network, and how many connections each person has. They formalized the idea of “small-world networks”; systems where most people are not directly connected, but the path between any two people is surprisingly short. As the population grows, distance increases very slowly. As the average number of connections grows, distance collapses quickly.

The paper highlights a critical theorem from random graph theory, which considers a network of $n$ individuals where each pair of nodes is connected with some probability. Let $k$ represent the average number of connections per person—the typical number of friends, acquaintances, or meaningful social ties an individual maintains.

The theorem states that when $k > 1$, a giant component emerges: a single, massive cluster in which a large fraction of the network becomes interconnected through chains of friends. Below this point, the network exists as scattered, disconnected islands. Just above it, the network “snaps together,” and a small increase in connectivity dramatically increases the number of indirect paths linking people.

As $k$ grows further, approaching $k > \ln(n)$, the network becomes almost surely fully connected, meaning everyone can reach everyone else through friends of friends. In practical terms, these thresholds explain how small changes in social behavior or connectivity can produce sudden, system-wide shifts in how information or influence spreads through a society.

In practical terms, even in a population of hundreds of millions, if each person knows around thirty others, the average distance between any two people is under six steps. Scale that up to most of the world’s population and the number barely changes. The system doesn’t stretch, it folds inward. That is the core insight: large networks don’t feel small by chance, they were engineered by the invisible arithmetic of human connections.
