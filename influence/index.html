Influence Is Not Popularity

A Marketing Reading of Kempe, Kleinberg, and Tardos

At the height of the early-2000s obsession with viral marketing, Kempe, Kleinberg, and Tardos wrote a paper that quietly dismantled one of marketing's most persistent myths: that the most influential people are simply the most connected ones. Maximizing the Spread of Influence through a Social Network argues that influence is not about volume or visibility, but about reach without redundancy—about finding the people who expose a message to new audiences, not the same crowd over and over again.

The paper frames influence as an optimization problem. Given a limited budget—say, free products, early access, or paid seeding—which small set of people should you target so that adoption spreads as widely as possible through a network? This question matters in marketing precisely because budgets are finite and audiences overlap. Two influencers with massive followings may look powerful individually, but if they reach the same audience, one of them is largely wasted spend.

Two Models of Influence Diffusion

To study this, the authors use two classic diffusion models. In the Linear Threshold model, people adopt when enough of their peers have adopted, reflecting social pressure and conformity. In the Independent Cascade model, adoption spreads probabilistically, more like word-of-mouth sparks jumping from person to person. Despite their differences, both models reveal the same structural truth: influence grows with diminishing returns. Each additional seed contributes less than the last if it overlaps with people already reached.

The Power of Submodularity

This property—called submodularity—turns out to be the key insight. It allows the authors to prove that a simple greedy strategy works remarkably well: select people one at a time, always choosing the next person who adds the most new reach. This strategy is guaranteed to achieve at least about 63% of the optimal possible spread, even though finding the true optimum is computationally infeasible. More importantly, this greedy approach naturally avoids the trap of over-investing in clustered, highly connected individuals.

The greedy algorithm doesn't just perform well—it fundamentally changes how we think about influence. Instead of chasing celebrity, it seeks structural diversity.

Why Traditional Heuristics Fail

When tested on large real-world networks, this method consistently outperforms common marketing heuristics like targeting high-degree users or socially "central" figures. These traditional strategies fail because they confuse visibility with influence. Central people often sit inside dense clusters; targeting many of them just reinforces the same audience. The greedy approach, by contrast, actively seeks structural diversity—people who bridge communities, operate at network boundaries, or connect otherwise separate groups.

Implications for Modern Marketing

For real-world marketing, the implications are direct and still highly relevant. Influencer marketing, referral programs, early-access launches, and even B2B sales seeding all face the same structural problem: audience overlap. The paper provides a theoretical foundation for why strategies that prioritize incremental reach—rather than raw follower counts—perform better. This logic shows up today in practices like lookalike modeling, frequency capping, creator portfolio diversification, and network-aware media planning, even when practitioners don't explicitly name the math behind it.

Ultimately, the paper's message is almost anti-glamorous. Influence doesn't live at the center of the network, surrounded by noise and repetition. It lives in the margins—in connectors, bridges, and underappreciated nodes that carry messages into new territory. Good marketing, the paper suggests, is not about shouting louder, but about placing your voice where it hasn't been heard yet.

Key Takeaway: The most influential nodes in a network are not necessarily the most visible ones. Strategic influence maximization requires identifying bridges and connectors who can reach new audiences, not just amplifying messages to the same dense clusters.
